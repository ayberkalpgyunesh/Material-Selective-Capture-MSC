2 · Tolerance ℓ: how picky docking is

ℓ is the width of the fit curve. It says how much mismatch the PSD still accepts.

Small ℓ: only very close granules fit. The PSD is picky and selection is strong.

Large ℓ: almost everything fits. A and B look the same to the PSD and selection disappears.

Dashed curve: productive use has a narrower tolerance ℓq (here 0.55 × ℓ), so using a granule is pickier than docking it. In the model ℓ is not measured; it is a chosen parameter (1.0–2.2).

7 · Lock-and-key or many weak contacts?

Conceptual illustration. This section is a back-of-the-envelope argument for the material-fit idea; it is not part of the manuscript analysis.

There are two ways a PSD could hold on to a granule. Both can hold it equally tight. The difference only shows when the synapse changes, and when there are many kinds of granules.

A · The synapse changes a little

softerno changestiffer

How long the granule stays at the PSD (starts at 10 s for both)

Why: 20 contacts × ~1 kT each. If each contact gets 10% weaker, the total drops by 2 kT, and staying time scales with eenergy: e2 ≈ 7. The single bond is a fixed protein pair, so the synapse cannot tune it without making a new protein.

B · There are many kinds of granules

A granule often carries only 1–2 copies of an mRNA. What makes it different is the set of RNA-binding proteins on it, and each of these proteins binds hundreds of mRNAs. So every granule is a combination:

What this shows, and what it does not

Activity cancels out. At one synapse all granules share the same T and M. So for two granules the docking ratio is about ΦA/ΦB = exp(−(d²A−d²B)/(2ℓ²)). Activity changes how many granules dock. Only the distance decides which ones.

The rule is assumed, the data are not. “Close means fit” is the MSC assumption. That dendritic mRNAs have higher G scores comes from real sequences. If they had lower scores, the WT PSD would prefer the wrong mRNAs.
